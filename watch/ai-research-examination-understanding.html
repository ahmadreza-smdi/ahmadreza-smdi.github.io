AI wrote the result. People still need to understand it.

Zara, a fictional research coordinator, receives an AI-generated result. A polished answer alone does not explain which assumptions support it, what was checked, or what its limitations mean.

Zara runs a fictional research conservatory. One morning, an AI system rolls out a long new manuscript, with a surprising result. It looks impressive. But a result can be impressive before anyone understands what it changes. OpenAI recently reported research manuscripts produced with its models.

It describes them as a form of raw research data, which still has to be turned into scientific understanding. OpenAI says outside expert groups are involved, and that it is trying different ways of releasing the work. This film doesn’t judge those results. It’s about

what has to happen next. In Zara’s conservatory, the manuscript moves through three workbenches. The first checks the explicit steps. Does each one follow the stated rules? Careful checking can confirm that, but only for the rules that were written down. The second examines the

assumptions. What was taken for granted? Does it hold in the real setting people care about? The third explains the implications. What does this change, for whom, and what does it leave unchanged? Here’s a simple puzzle card we invented. It isn’t a real paper.

Every move follows the rules. But the rules assume the board never changes, and checking the moves can’t tell you whether that’s true. These are different tasks. A checked step is not the same as an understood result. So when an AI result arrives, ask

three things. What exactly is claimed? What checks were done? And what remains uncertain? Impressive comes first. Understanding takes people, time and careful examination. Ahmadreza Samadi. More at a-samadi.com
