How much income goes to gross rent?

An ACS 2024 snapshot across 386 metropolitan areas in the 50 states and District of Columbia. The 30% and 50% thresholds describe renter-occupied units with a computable gross-rent-to-income ratio. Units outside that denominator stay visible.

By Ahmadreza Samadi · Source snapshot checked 17 October 2026

What does this study measure?

It calculates the share of renter-occupied units whose Census-tabulated gross rent is at least 30% or at least 50% of household income, among units for which that ratio can be computed. Gross rent includes contract rent and separately renter-paid utilities and fuels. The 50% group is part of the 30% group; the two shares must not be added.

This is a descriptive survey snapshot. It does not establish an individual household's full budget, a property's yield, a future rent, a causal explanation or an investment recommendation.

Who is in the denominator?

The table's universe is renter-occupied housing units. Its “Not computed” category includes units where no rent is paid and households reporting no income or a net loss. Excluding that category from a ratio does not establish that those households have low costs or no affordability difficulty.

For each published whole metropolitan area:

  • Computable denominator = total renter-occupied units minus not-computed units.
  • At least 30% numerator = the 30.0–34.9, 35.0–39.9, 40.0–49.9 and 50.0-or-more percentage bins.
  • At least 50% numerator = the 50.0-or-more bin.
  • Each share = 100 × its numerator divided by the computable denominator.

B25070: denominator=E001−E011;30%+numerator=E007+E008+E009+E010;50%+numerator=E010.

The CSV also reports not-computed units as a share of all renter-occupied units. A zero denominator produces an unavailable percentage; missing or sentinel estimates are not converted to zero. The cutoffs refer to the published rounded ratio bins, not a new calculation from household microdata.

A fictional denominator check

Imagine 100 renter-occupied units: 20 are not computed, 40 are in the at-least 30% bins and 10 are in the at-least 50% bin. The denominator is 80. The two shares are 50% and 12.5%. Dividing 40 by 100 would instead produce 40%, answering a different question. These are invented teaching counts, not a metro's data.

Why 386 metros?

The pipeline joins official ACS 1-year table B25070 to its matching geography labels by exact GEO_ID. It selects whole metropolitan statistical areas at summary level 310 and component 00, retaining cross-state areas once. It excludes 140 published micropolitan rows and 6 Puerto Rico metropolitan rows from this 50 states/DC scope. All excluded CBSA rows and reasons are retained.

The 1-year product does not publish every small geography. No missing metro is filled with 5-year estimates. This is a different source universe from the earlier HUD rent/ownership study; the number of rows should not be forced to match it.

What should a reader keep uncertain?

  • Sampling uncertainty. ACS figures are estimates. All 11 published estimate/MOE pairs are preserved in the CSV. Derived percentage MOEs are not calculated; this page does not rank statistical differences.
  • Time. The data year is 2024. Rent and household income have different ACS measurement windows. This is not a live asking-rent feed or a forecast.
  • Households outside the ratio. Not-computed units remain visible, rather than being called zero burden.
  • Individual decisions. A metropolitan share does not assess a particular apartment, household, loan or property return.

As checked 17 October 2026, the official 2025 ACS 1-year release is announced for 29 October 2026. No 2025 values are used here. The source date and availability will be checked at that announced release, with the saved 2024 snapshot retained for reproducibility.
