Profitable. But out of cash: why payment timing matters

A profitable sale can leave a business unable to pay its supplier. Follow one order to see why earned money and available cash are different.

By Ahmadreza Samadi · 8 October 2026

Watch the film

90-second animated film with English narration and subtitles. The business and all transaction amounts are fictional. Tax and other expenses are excluded. Narration is AI-generated.

One profitable order

A small business starts with $4,000 in its bank account. It delivers a $10,000 order on Day 1. The products cost $7,000, obtained on supplier credit. With the sale delivered and its cost recognised, the simplified profit is $3,000 before tax and other expenses. The invoice records what the customer owes; it does not add cash to the bank.

The dates create the problem

The supplier's $7,000 payment is due on Day 15. The customer has agreed to pay on Day 60. On Day 15, the business has only $4,000 available, leaving a $3,000 cash shortfall. The customer is not even late. The business needs to cover the gap before the agreed customer payment arrives.

Profit and cash answer different questions

Profit measures revenue less recognised expenses over a period. Cash flow tracks money actually moving in and out. Under accrual accounting, earning revenue and collecting payment can happen at different times. The SEC's cash-flow explainer describes how a cash-flow statement connects income to actual cash movements. A shortfall is not automatically bankruptcy, but an unresolved inability to meet payments can threaten a business's survival.

What the recent research tells us

Intuit's 2026 reports say 39% of surveyed US owners experienced payroll or bill pressure from a single late payment in the previous year. The underlying owner survey covered 1,305 US owners in December 2025. It reports their experience, not a worldwide business-failure rate. This research concerns late payments; the fictional film example shows that agreed payment terms alone can also create a cash gap.

A deposit changes timing, not profit

In the film's alternative terms, the customer pays a $4,000 deposit before delivery and the remaining $6,000 on Day 60. Opening cash plus the deposit gives $8,000 available. Paying the supplier $7,000 leaves $1,000. The total sale remains $10,000 and simplified profit remains $3,000. The deposit is part of the sale, not extra revenue. This illustrates one possible agreement, not a promise that a customer will accept it or that the buffer covers every business expense.

Three questions for a founder or investor

More orders can increase cash needed upfront when suppliers must be paid before customers pay. Map the dates, consider deposits or better payment terms where feasible, and plan a cash cushion. Before judging a business by profit alone, ask: When does cash arrive? What is due first? What covers the gap?
