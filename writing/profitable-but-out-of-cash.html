How can a profitable business run out of cash?

A business can recognize a profitable sale before the customer pays, while a supplier bill comes due earlier. The fictional order below shows a 3,000-dollar simplified profit and a 3,000-dollar cash gap on the supplier payment date. The practical check is the dated bank balance, including what is due before the receipt arrives.

Key points

  • An invoice is not money in the bank.
  • Map payment dates as well as revenue and expense amounts.
  • Test the lowest cash balance before accepting another commitment.

How can an order show a profit before cash arrives?

A small business starts with $4,000 in its bank account. It delivers a $10,000 order on Day 1. The products cost $7,000, obtained on supplier credit. With the sale delivered and its cost recognised, the simplified profit is $3,000 before tax and other expenses. The invoice records what the customer owes; it does not add cash to the bank.

Why does cash run short before the customer pays?

The supplier's $7,000 payment is due on Day 15. The customer has agreed to pay on Day 60. On Day 15, the business has only $4,000 available, leaving a $3,000 cash shortfall. The customer is not even late. The business needs to cover the gap before the agreed customer payment arrives.

Profit and cash answer different questions

Profit measures revenue less recognised expenses over a period. Cash flow tracks money actually moving in and out. Under accrual accounting, earning revenue and collecting payment can happen at different times. The SEC's cash-flow explainer describes how a cash-flow statement connects income to actual cash movements. A shortfall is not automatically bankruptcy, but an unresolved inability to meet payments can threaten a business's survival.

What the recent research tells us

Intuit's 2026 reports say 39% of surveyed US owners experienced payroll or bill pressure from a single late payment in the previous year. The underlying owner survey covered 1,305 US owners in December 2025. It reports their experience, not a worldwide business-failure rate. This research concerns late payments; the fictional film example shows that agreed payment terms alone can also create a cash gap.

A deposit changes timing, not profit

In the film's alternative terms, the customer pays a $4,000 deposit before delivery and the remaining $6,000 on Day 60. Opening cash plus the deposit gives $8,000 available. Paying the supplier $7,000 leaves $1,000. The total sale remains $10,000 and simplified profit remains $3,000. The deposit is part of the sale, not extra revenue. This illustrates one possible agreement, not a promise that a customer will accept it or that the buffer covers every business expense.

Three questions for a founder or investor

More orders can increase cash needed upfront when suppliers must be paid before customers pay. Map the dates, consider deposits or better payment terms where feasible, and plan a cash cushion. Before judging a business by profit alone, ask: When does cash arrive? What is due first? What covers the gap?

How do you turn the example into a dated cash plan?

List the opening bank balance and each expected receipt and payment by the date cash is expected to move. Keep an invoice date, contractual due date and actual settlement date in separate columns. Mark an estimate as an estimate. A customer promise is not an actual receipt, and a balance that depends on that promise should stay conditional until the money arrives.

Run the balance forward after each event. The useful warning in the fictional order is the Day 15 funding gap, not the eventual total profit. The table deliberately shows the gap rather than pretending the business can make a payment with unavailable funds. Before extending the example, specify how the gap would be covered and what that funding would cost.

Now add the payments the teaching example excludes: payroll, rent, tax, other suppliers and any existing borrowing. Use the actual amounts and dates for your business. A deposit that covers one supplier bill may leave another commitment uncovered. Reconcile the plan to the bank account and retain unsettled items rather than treating the forecast as an account balance.

Compare the current terms with one feasible alternative, such as staged customer payments or a different supplier due date. Record whether that alternative has actually been agreed. Do not count an unapproved deposit as cash available. Growing sales can increase the amount tied up between delivery and collection; repeat the dated plan for the new orders instead of multiplying profit and assuming the bank balance follows.

The decision is operational: which commitment can be met, on which date, using which confirmed source of cash? This proposed planning method is educational and does not replace the business’s bookkeeping or professional accounting advice. It reports no personal transaction or company outcome.
