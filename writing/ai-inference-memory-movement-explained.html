Why does moving data matter for AI chips?

Microsoft’s 12 October Maia announcement highlights data movement in AI infrastructure. Here is the idea, without repeating its performance forecasts.

By Ahmadreza Samadi · 14 October 2026

Training learns. Inference uses.

Training adjusts a model. Inference uses a trained model to produce a result from an input. Today’s chip story focuses on inference.

Ask three workload questions.

What model and input size? How long until a useful reply? How much complete-system energy and cost? Keep the output quality requirement the same.

The chip needs the next pieces.

A computing unit performs operations. Model data and working information must reach it from memory. Waiting for those pieces can limit useful work.

Source: IBM: What is AI inference?

A nearby shelf is a useful analogy.

Imagine a cook and ingredients. A fast cook can still wait on deliveries. Nearby storage can reduce some trips. This is an analogy, not a chip diagram to scale.

Source: IBM: What is AI inference?

More requests change the problem.

Serving many users also involves memory, communication and scheduling. The whole system matters; one headline chip number does not describe every workload.

Source: IBM: What is AI inference?

Today and 2027 are separate.

Microsoft says Maia 200 serves production workloads. It plans Maia 300 production deployments in 2027. A plan is not evidence of deployment today.
