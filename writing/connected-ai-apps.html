Connected AI apps: access, scope and approval

When an AI assistant asks you to connect an app, you are deciding what it can see and what it can change. That decision deserves a minute of attention.

By Ahmadreza Samadi · 25 September 2026

Updated 10 October 2026

At a glance

Connecting an app gives an AI assistant access within a specific account, permission scope and integration. Reading information is different from creating, editing, sending or deleting it; supported actions and confirmation rules vary. Before connecting, check which account is used, what can be read or changed, when approval is required and how to revoke access.

AI assistants are connecting to more of the tools people use every day: calendars, email, files, notes and task lists. On 23 September 2026, Google announced a new wave of Connected Apps for Gemini, and OpenAI documents a similar capability in ChatGPT. The feature is useful, but the single word “connect” hides several separate decisions.

What is a connected app?

A connected app is an authorized path between an AI assistant and another service. Instead of copying your meetings into a chat, you let the assistant look them up through the account and access you approve. Ask “what is on my calendar tomorrow?” and a supported calendar connection can answer without you pasting every event.

Without that connection, the assistant cannot look up that app’s information through the integration. With it, the assistant can use the app within the limits of that particular integration.

What is the difference between reading and acting?

The most useful distinction is between reading and acting. Finding a meeting is different from creating one. Summarizing a document is different from editing it. Some integrations only read; others can also take supported actions, and many ask for confirmation first.

A connector’s existence does not mean an assistant can do everything in that app. OpenAI’s documentation says the available data and actions depend on the app, the access granted when you connect, and any workspace controls. No announcement promises that every app can both read and write.

Which four boundaries limit what an assistant can do?

  1. The account. Which account did you connect, personal or work? The assistant can only reach what that account can reach.
  2. The scope. What access did you grant at connection time? Read-only access and permission to make changes are different grants.
  3. The integration. What does this specific connector support? Two apps in the same category can offer very different capabilities.
  4. The approval rules. When does the assistant ask before acting? A clear confirmation step matters most for anything that sends, deletes or changes records.

How a request moves through a connected app

From a product architecture point of view, I think about four checkpoints:

  1. Understand the user’s request.
  2. Select the connected tool only if it is needed.
  3. Check the account, scope and supported capability.
  4. Return information, or follow the approval rules for a supported action.

Each checkpoint is a place where a well-designed system can stop, ask or explain. That is what makes an integration trustworthy enough for real work.

Three questions to ask before you connect any app

  • Which account? Make sure it is the one you intend to use for this task.
  • What can it read? Know which records, files or messages become available.
  • What can it change? Find out whether it can create, edit, send or delete, and when it asks first.

Also check how to revoke access. Connect for a clear purpose, review access from time to time, and confirm before anything changes. These habits keep the benefit without the surprise.

What this means for teams building AI products

For product and engineering teams, the same boundaries are design decisions. Request the narrowest access the use case needs, make the difference between reading and acting visible to the user, and treat the approval step as part of the product rather than an afterthought. Clear use cases and clear boundaries make integrations more useful, not less.
