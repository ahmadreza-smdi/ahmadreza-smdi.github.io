Rent or buy: the five numbers that decide it

By Ahmadreza Samadi · 20 October 2026

Start with five scenario inputs: the cost of comparable rented housing, the ownership cash budget, the cash required upfront, the expected holding period, and the cash left after selling and repaying debt. They make the financial comparison inspectable; they do not predict house prices or decide which home fits your life. Compare cash affordability separately from the wealth you may retain.

Key points

  • Compare homes that provide similar housing, not unrelated market averages.
  • Principal repayment uses cash but may build equity; do not count it twice.
  • Model the sale and a shorter holding period before treating ownership as affordable.

What would comparable rented housing cost?

Begin with the home you would actually rent instead. Match location, size, usable condition and the period being considered. Record rent, recurring tenant charges and utilities on a consistent basis. If utilities are included in one option but excluded in the other, a headline comparison can be misleading before any mortgage calculation starts. Keep refundable deposits separate from consumed costs, while still recording when that cash is tied up.

Use a dated quote or current lease as the starting observation. Future rent changes are assumptions rather than facts. Make them visible and examine alternatives instead of hiding a single growth rate in a final answer. A home that costs less but provides a materially different commute, space or flexibility is not an equivalent alternative just because both amounts are monthly.

The 393-metro study on this site is useful for exploring area-level ownership cash scenarios and their assumptions. It does not compare a specific rental with the same property offered for sale. Its HUD and Census benchmarks describe different populations and measures. Use it to understand the model and formulate questions; replace area averages with matched property evidence before making a personal decision.

What is the complete ownership cash budget?

Build an annual budget from the actual financing terms and property obligations. Separate principal, interest, taxes or local charges, insurance, building or association charges, maintenance and other applicable expenses. Record which amounts are already included in the payment quoted by the lender. The CFPB explains that a mortgage payment can include more than principal and interest; adding those included items again would double-count them.

Cash affordability and economic cost are different views of this budget. Principal repayment reduces cash available now, but it also reduces debt. Interest and consumed maintenance do not have the same effect. A comparison that calls every mortgage payment a pure expense misses equity; a comparison that ignores principal because it is not a consumed cost can still leave the household unable to make its payments.

The rental cash-flow calculator can help examine financing and operating components for a rental-property scenario. It is an investor tool, not a complete owner-occupier rent-versus-buy model. Do not treat hypothetical rent received in that calculator as a personal housing saving. For this decision, keep a separate household cash budget and a separate equity or wealth reconciliation.

How much cash is required before you move in?

List the down payment, purchase and financing fees, moving costs, immediate work and cash reserves separately. Distinguish cash paid towards the asset from fees consumed in making the transaction. For a Dubai scenario, the purchase-cost calculator helps organise buyer-paid components using dated fee references and explicit payer allocations. It cannot replace the agreement or the transaction-specific fee quote.

The CFPB’s Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure explainers distinguish loan information, closing costs and the amount needed to close in the US mortgage process. They are useful document-reading examples rather than UAE fee schedules. For any jurisdiction, reconcile the documents appropriate to that transaction and record who pays each component. Avoid importing a familiar percentage from an unrelated purchase.

Also ask what this cash would otherwise do. Money used for a down payment cannot simultaneously remain available for emergencies or another use. An alternative investment return is uncertain and must be modelled as an assumption, after any relevant costs. A scenario that assumes growth on the full initial cash and also leaves that cash available has created money twice.

How long do you expect to stay?

A holding period turns upfront and exit costs into a meaningful comparison. A fee paid once is spread across very different housing use when you stay for two years rather than ten. But simply dividing it by the expected years is not a complete investment calculation: payment timing, financing changes and sale proceeds still matter. Keep the year-by-year cash movements visible.

Run at least a shorter-stay scenario as a practical sensitivity check. A job change, family need or property issue may require a move before the planned date. This is an editorial planning suggestion, not a statistical probability of moving. The goal is to see which assumption changes the conclusion and whether the resulting cash requirement is manageable.

If you do not know the holding period, leave it as a range of scenarios. A precise break-even year produced from one uncertain price-growth assumption is not a forecast. The useful question is whether the choice remains workable under several plausible conditions, including no price growth, higher running costs or a different refinancing outcome.

What cash would remain after a sale?

Calculate net exit cash as the sale proceeds minus selling costs, remaining debt and any other applicable settlement amounts. It is not the full sale price. Show the balance of the loan at that date and confirm whether any financing or transaction charges apply. Sale price growth is an input assumption; it is not established by today’s valuation estimate or an area-level historical series.

Consider a fictional three-year cash comparison. Renting costs 12,000 currency units a year, or 36,000 in total. Buying requires 60,000 upfront and 18,000 a year in ownership cash payments. If net sale cash after debt and selling costs is 65,000, the net ownership cash outflow is 60,000 +54,000 −65,000 =49,000. This simple scenario excludes discounting, alternative investment returns and differences in housing quality.

If part of those annual payments repays principal, its effect is already reflected in the remaining debt used to calculate net exit cash. Do not subtract that principal again from the 49,000 result. Equally, do not describe the assumed 65,000 as a promised recovery. Changing the exit assumption or time horizon can reverse the comparison.

Finish with two conclusions rather than a single buy-or-rent verdict: can the household fund the cash path, and how sensitive is the financial comparison to the assumptions? Then consider flexibility and the home itself. The five numbers organise the evidence and trade-offs. They cannot remove uncertainty or substitute for transaction-specific financial and legal advice.
