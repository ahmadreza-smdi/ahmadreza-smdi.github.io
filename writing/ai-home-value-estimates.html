AI property valuation: what an estimate misses

AI property valuation estimates a home’s value from the property and market data available to a model. Check the facts, comparable sales and uncertainty before using the number. Zillow describes its Zestimate as an estimate, not an appraisal.

By Ahmadreza Samadi · 3 October 2026 · Updated 20 October 2026

Key points

  • Check the property identity, record date and physical condition.
  • Compare suitable sales and the uncertainty around the estimate.
  • Investigate conflicting valuations before choosing or averaging them.

What does AI property valuation estimate?

The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes an automated valuation model, or AVM, as a computer-generated estimate that compares property facts such as bedrooms, bathrooms and floor area with recent sales and other local market information. Different valuations can use different comparable sales, dates or purposes. A precise-looking figure does not make those inputs complete.

Zillow's Zestimate explanation gives a concrete product example: its model uses public records, listing-service data and details supplied by users, as well as market trends. Zillow says an unreported renovation may not be reflected and that incorrect or incomplete home facts can affect accuracy. That describes Zillow's product; other AVMs may use different data and methods.

What can an automated estimate miss?

Changes in condition and gaps in the records can be absent from the estimate.

Imagine two illustrative homes with the same recorded size and bedroom count. One has a recently renovated kitchen; the other needs major roof work. If those conditions are absent from the available records, the shared facts alone cannot tell the full story. A weak set of comparable sales can add another source of error. This is why I would check the underlying facts and comparisons before acting on an estimate.

A single point estimate also compresses uncertainty. Zillow publishes an estimated sales range alongside its Zestimate and says the final sale price may vary with negotiation and market conditions. Its own explanation explicitly says the Zestimate is not an appraisal. Treat the range and the evidence behind the number as part of the answer.

Can valuation errors differ across communities?

Yes, but a finding applies to the model, data and places actually studied.

There is a broader quality question beyond one house. A 2025 Urban Institute study of US AVMs found that valuation errors and undervaluation can differ across homeowner groups in its studied markets, even after accounting for property and neighborhood characteristics. That result is not a measured claim about every model, country or property. It is a reason to test error by relevant groups and locations, not only report one overall accuracy score.

The cited Urban study uses 2018 records from selected counties around Atlanta and Memphis and imputes homeowner race. It does not measure every AVM or market.

Three checks before relying on an estimate

  1. Are the property facts current? Confirm size, rooms, renovations and condition against reliable records or inspection.
  2. Which sales are genuinely comparable? Consider location, timing, property type and important differences, not just a nearby sale price.
  3. What is missing, and how uncertain is the result? Look for a range, review the evidence, and seek professional valuation when the decision warrants it.

What should I do when two valuations disagree?

Use the property comparables evidence checklist to examine the source, match and gaps behind a comparison.

The CFPB explanation notes that valuations can differ because of comparable sales, timing or purpose. My proposed review sequence is:

  1. Check the property and the question. Confirm the property identity, valuation date and purpose for each estimate. Explain differences in dates or purposes before comparing the figures.
  2. Inspect the inputs. Compare the recorded property facts, condition information and cited sales. Keep missing or conflicting information visible.
  3. Resolve the difference or retain the uncertainty. Investigate the evidence before selecting the higher figure or treating an average as a verified valuation. Seek a relevant professional valuation when the decision warrants it.

Averaging two numbers does not, by itself, reconcile conflicting property facts. This is a proposed way to organize a review; its usefulness depends on reliable evidence and the purpose of the valuation.
